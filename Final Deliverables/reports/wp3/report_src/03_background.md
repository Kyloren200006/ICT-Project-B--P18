# 3. Background and related work

This section is a targeted review, not a systematic one: it covers the benchmarks and standards that bear on how WP3 defines and computes trust scores. WP1 holds the systematic literature review that underlies the risk taxonomy. Descriptions below are limited to what each source's title and abstract state; where a design choice of WP3's differs from a source, the difference is stated as a difference in purpose, not as a flaw.

## 3.1 Existing approaches to measuring agent safety

Recent benchmarks each measure one slice of agent risk. AgentDojo [@debenedetti2024agentdojo] is a dynamic environment for evaluating prompt injection attacks and defences against tool-using agents. InjecAgent [@zhan2024injecagent] benchmarks indirect prompt injection in tool-integrated agents. Agent Security Bench [@zhang2024asb] formalises and benchmarks attacks and defences on agents across scenarios. AgentHarm [@andriushchenko2024agentharm] measures how harmful agents are when given harmful multi-step tasks. PrivacyLens [@shao2024privacylens] evaluates privacy norm awareness of language models in action, which is the nearest prior work to WP3's leakage metrics. ToolEmu [@ruan2024toolemu] identifies the risks of tool-using agents by running them in a language-model-emulated sandbox with a language-model safety evaluator.

Three things distinguish what WP3 was asked to build from these. First, it reports one trust profile across several risks at once (injection, leakage, unsafe actions, excessive agency, memory exposure), so a reader can compare agents on all of them with the same scale. Second, it scores from a fixed run-log format, so any agent that can emit the log can be scored without access to its internals. Third, it must run with no manual steps and give identical results every time, which is what the guide requires of the engine. These differences are why WP3 builds its own metrics and engine instead of reusing a published one; they are not a claim that the published work is weaker, and several of the sources are good models for the scenario side (WP2) and the attack catalogue (WP1).

## 3.2 Standards and frameworks

The risk categories come from WP1's threat taxonomy (TC1 to TC6) and, through it, from the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications [@owasp_llm_top10], whose entries for Prompt Injection, Sensitive Information Disclosure and Excessive Agency are cited in the metric definitions. MITRE ATLAS [@mitre_atlas] is the adversary-tactics knowledge base that WP1 draws on for attack techniques. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework [@nist_ai_rmf] frames measurement as one of four functions of AI risk management and is the context in which a trust benchmark sits. The privacy rationale for Memory Isolation and Data Minimisation cites the Australian Privacy Principles (APP 6 on use and disclosure, APP 11 on security of personal information) [@app_oaic], since the project is run in Australia. The principle behind Data Minimisation and Excessive Agency, that a component should have no more access than its task needs, is least privilege from Saltzer and Schroeder [@saltzer1975]. The metric definitions also cite two works for the attack side: Greshake et al. [@greshake2023] on indirect prompt injection and Wei, Haghtalab and Steinhardt [@wei2023jailbroken] on why safety training fails; and Bhosale et al. [@bhosale2026] for autonomous-agent risks.

## 3.3 Methods used for the validation

Agreement between the engine and hand labels is reported as accuracy, precision, recall and Cohen's kappa [@cohen1960]. Kappa corrects raw agreement for the agreement expected by chance, which matters here because most cells are "no failure" and a rule that never flags anything would still look accurate. The reasons for not using a language model as the scorer rely on published evidence that model judges show biases such as position and verbosity preference [@zheng2023judge].
